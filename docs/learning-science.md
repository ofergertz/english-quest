# What the research says: game mechanics that teach young EFL learners best

> Research brief (October 2026) for Hebrew-speaking children, grades 1–6, pilot grade 4.
> Most strong evidence comes from cognitive-psychology studies with native-language or adult learners; evidence for young EFL learners specifically is thinner, and each section says so. Papers seen only via a secondary summary are marked *(secondary)*.

## A. Ranked activity types

| # | Mechanic | Evidence | Best for | Concrete example in the game |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Retrieval practice + feedback** | Strong (moderate for young kids) | All grades | Hear "banana" → tap the banana among 4 pictures → correct item glows and the word is replayed |
| 2 | **Spaced review across days** | Strong, incl. children | All | "apple" learned Sunday reappears Monday, Wednesday, next Sunday, two weeks later |
| 3 | **Systematic phonics / phonemic awareness** | Strong (moderate for EFL) | Sounds only gr. 1–3; letters+CVC gr. 3–4 | Hear /s/ /u/ /n/ → drag s-u-n → sun picture rises |
| 4 | **Dual coding: picture + audio + print** | Moderate–strong | All, essential for non-readers | Every word card = clean picture + native audio + printed word (print from grade 3–4) |
| 5 | **Gesture / TPR** | Moderate | Gr. 1–3, verbs/adjectives | "Jump!" — the guide jumps, the child jumps too, then taps the jumping picture |
| 6 | **Listen-and-do comprehension** | Moderate | Gr. 1–4 | "Put the red ball under the table" → drag the ball into the scene |
| 7 | **Minimal-pair listening** | Moderate (older kids), weak under 8 | Gr. 4–6 | "Which did you hear: *ship* or *sheep*?" / *three* or *tree*? |
| 8 | **Mastery-gated progression** | Strong | All | Next station unlocks only at ~80–90% across two sessions; weak words recycle |
| 9 | **Adaptive difficulty** | Moderate–strong | All | Aim ~80–85% success: 2 → 4 picture choices, remove the audio hint as the child improves |
| 10 | **Elaborated feedback** | Strong | All | Wrong pick "ship" for "sheep" → both shown side by side, "sh-ee-p, long *ee*" |
| 11 | **Decodable micro-stories with read-along** | Moderate | Gr. 3–6 | 4-sentence story using only taught words; tap any word to hear it |
| 12 | **Narrative wrapper** | Moderate (motivation) | All | Each unit is a world the guide character explores; stations unlock story beats |
| 13 | **Sight-word recognition sprints** | Moderate | Gr. 3–6 | "the, is, my, I, you" flash — beat *your own* best, optional timer |
| 14 | **Songs and chants** | Weak for learning, fine for motivation | Gr. 1–3 | Short chant at unit start with the 6 target words |
| 15 | **Speech-recognition pronunciation** | Moderate for adults, unproven for kids; high technical risk | Gr. 4–6, known words only | "Say *apple*" → play back the child's recording next to the model; never block progress |

Key numbers behind the table:
- Testing effect in classrooms: g = 0.50 overall, 0.33 in elementary, 0.54 with corrective feedback, larger with 3+ quizzes (Yang et al. 2021).
- Spacing with grade-3 vocabulary: three sessions once each beat one session three times (Goossens et al. 2012). L2 delayed tests: spaced g = 1.71 vs massed 0.58 (Webb et al. 2023).
- Phonics: d = 0.41, 0.55 when started early (Ehri et al. 2001). EFL synthesis: moderate gains in phonemic awareness and non-word reading, weaker for comprehension (Huo & Wang 2017).
- Pictures and gestures with 8-year-olds learning English: both beat verbal-only, retained at 6 months; gestures did not beat pictures for children (Andrä et al. 2020).
- Mastery learning: ES 0.52, higher thresholds better (Kulik et al. 1990). Adaptive tutors: g ≈ 0.41–0.57 (Ma et al. 2014).
- Feedback: elaborated 0.49, correct-answer only 0.32, right/wrong only 0.05 (Van der Kleij et al. 2015).
- Child speech recognition error ~15–21% (ages 6–10); non-native children reading aloud ~25% (Wills et al. 2023).

## B. Mechanics to avoid or limit

| Avoid | Why | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Expected prizes for *doing* the task ("10 coins per game") | Reduces intrinsic motivation, worse for children (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999) | Unexpected, informational recognition: "Now you can read 20 words!", story unlocks, visible progress |
| Leaderboards / ranking | Lower motivation and scores; bottom-ranked kids disengage (Hanus & Fox 2015; Nicholson 2013) | Personal-best tracking ("Yesterday 7, today 9!") |
| Busy visuals, irrelevant animations always on screen | Seductive details harm learning (Sundararajan & Adesope 2020); decorated rooms → more off-task (Fisher et al. 2014) | Calm screens; animation only as feedback, then stop |
| Timers during first learning | Pressure before mastery | Optional timer only on mastered words, self-referenced |
| Teaching all members of one category at once (all colours together) | Semantic interference (Nakata & Suzuki 2019) | Mix categories; introduce confusables (b/d, left/right) on separate days, then contrast |
| Bare "Wrong!" | ES ≈ 0.05 | Show the right answer + replay + name the contrast |
| Blocking progress on speech recognition | Frequent false "wrong" on child voices | Self-comparison playback, no score |
| Expecting the app alone to teach reading | GraphoGame Rime RCT: no effect alone; g = 0.48 with adult involvement *(secondary)* | Parent touchpoints: weekly "read it to me" card, parent screen |

### About positive feedback
Praise should be warm **and informative**. Generic verbal praise had a small, non-significant effect on children's motivation (Deci et al. 1999, d = 0.11), while specific feedback is strongly effective. So every positive message names what went well:
- ✔ "Great listening — you heard the *sh* in *ship*!"
- ✔ "You read the whole sentence by yourself!"
- ✔ "Yesterday 6 words, today 9 — you're growing!"
- ✘ Only "Good job!" over and over.

After a mistake: encouraging, never disappointed — "Almost! Listen again: *sh-ee-p*." The guide character looks curious, never sad.

## C. Key design numbers

| Parameter | Value | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Retrievals per word | Plan 8–12, spread across sessions | Incidental learning needs ~6–20+ encounters *(secondary)*; deliberate retrieval needs fewer |
| Review schedule | same session → next day → ~3 days → ~1 week → 2–3 weeks → monthly | Synthesis of Cepeda 2008, Kim & Webb, Goossens 2012 — not tested as-is |
| Session length | Gr. 1–3: 10–15 min; gr. 4–6: 15–20 min, in short blocks | Effective doses in studies: 10 min/day (GraphoGame), 20 min/day × 5 (Andrä) |
| Target accuracy | ~80–85% | Heuristic (Wilson et al. 2019 model) + mastery research |
| Mastery threshold | ~80–90% across separate sessions | Kulik et al. 1990 |
| New words per session | ≤ 10–12 | Ministry guidance for elementary |

## D. Hebrew-speaker specifics

- **Vowels:** Hebrew has 5 vowels with no long/short distinction → *sit/seat, hat/hut, feel/fill* collapse. Make short vowels and silent-e an explicit strand with minimal pairs. Israeli grade-5 study: short vowels a weak point (Kahn-Horwitz, Schwartz & Share 2011).
- **Consonants:** no /θ/ /ð/ (*th* → t/d/s/z), *w* → v. Minimal pairs: *three/tree, west/vest, think/sink*.
- **Direction and reversals:** writing direction contributes to reversals (Fischer 2016). Add b/d/p/q discrimination games with anchor cues ("**b**ed": b on the left, d on the right), and a left-to-right highlight sweep while decoding. *(No study specifically on Hebrew readers — inference.)*
- **Hebrew literacy predicts English literacy** (145 Israeli 4th graders; Kahn-Horwitz, Shimron & Sparks 2005). A short Hebrew phonological-awareness check at onboarding can set phonics pacing.
- Israeli EFL textbooks cover phonics components poorly (Fuchs, Katzir & Kahn-Horwitz 2023) — a gap the game can fill.

## Gaps
No independent evaluation of Teach Your Monster to Read found; no child-EFL studies of shadowing; no experiment on b/d confusion specifically in Hebrew readers; no Israeli experiment on vowel-contrast training.
